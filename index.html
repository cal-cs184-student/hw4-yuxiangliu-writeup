Overview

In this homework I built a real-time cloth simulator based on a mass-spring model. The cloth is a grid of point masses connected by three kinds of springs (structural, shearing and bending). Every time step I add up gravity and Hooke's-law spring forces, advance each mass with damped Verlet integration, and then fix up the result: I push masses out of spheres and planes, push apart parts of the cloth that come too close to each other (using a spatial hash so this stays fast), and limit how far any spring can stretch (the Provot 10% rule).

The most interesting thing I learned is how much of a "physical" simulation is actually made up of geometric corrections rather than forces. Collisions, self-collisions and the 10% stretch limit all move positions directly, and because Verlet integration infers velocity from the last two positions, each of those corrections also changes the cloth's velocity. That is why the self-collision correction has to be spread out over the substeps, and why very soft cloth (low ks) ends up hanging on the 10% constraint instead of on its springs. I also learned that what you see at rest depends mostly on the ratio of weight to stiffness (density vs. ks), while damping changes only how the cloth moves on its way to rest, not the final shape.

Finally, I wrote GLSL shaders to render the cloth in real time: diffuse and Blinn-Phong lighting, texture mapping, bump and displacement mapping, and an environment-mapped mirror. The main lesson there was the split between the two shader stages. Anything computed per pixel (lighting, bump normals) looks detailed no matter how coarse the mesh is, while anything computed per vertex (displacement) can only be as detailed as the mesh.

Part 1: Masses and springs

Cloth::buildGrid builds the cloth in two passes. First it creates num_width_points × num_height_points point masses evenly spaced over width × height, in row-major order (mass \((x, y)\) is stored at index \(y \cdot \text{num_width_points} + x\)). A horizontal cloth lies in the \(xz\)-plane at \(y = 1\). A vertical cloth lies in the \(xy\)-plane with a tiny random \(z\) offset in \([-10^{-3}, 10^{-3}]\), so it is not perfectly flat and can start folding. A mass is pinned if its \((x, y)\) grid index appears in the scene's pinned list. I reserve the vector's capacity up front, because springs store raw pointers to masses and a reallocation would invalidate them.

After all masses exist, the second pass adds springs. Each mass only connects "backwards" (to the left and up), so every pair is connected exactly once:

  • Structural: to the mass on the left \((x-1, y)\) and the mass above \((x, y-1)\).
  • Shearing: to the two diagonal masses above, \((x-1, y-1)\) and \((x+1, y-1)\).
  • Bending: to the mass two to the left \((x-2, y)\) and two above \((x, y-2)\).

For the 50 × 50 cloth this gives 2500 masses, 4900 structural, 4802 shearing and 4800 bending springs. Each spring's rest length is the distance between its two masses at creation time. The screenshots below show scene/pinned2.json from above, with different spring types turned on.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

Each call to Cloth::simulate advances the cloth by one substep \(\Delta t = 1 / (\text{fps} \cdot \text{steps per frame}) = 1/2700\) s with the default settings:

  1. Forces. Every mass's force is reset to the external force \(F = m \sum a_\text{ext}\) (gravity), where \(m = \text{density} \cdot \text{width} \cdot \text{height} / N\). Then, for every spring whose type is enabled, Hooke's law \(F_s = k_s (\|p_a - p_b\| - l)\) is applied along the spring, with equal and opposite forces on its two ends. Bending springs use \(0.2\,k_s\), so they are weaker.
  2. Verlet integration. For every unpinned mass, \[ x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + (1 - d)(x_t - x_{t-\Delta t}) + \frac{F}{m}\Delta t^2, \qquad d = \text{damping}/100, \] and the old position is saved as last_position.
  3. Constraint (Provot 1995). For every spring longer than \(1.1\,l\), the two ends are pulled toward each other along the spring until it is exactly \(1.1\,l\) long. The correction is split evenly, or applied entirely to the unpinned end if the other end is pinned, and skipped if both ends are pinned.

Note: the GUI shows density in g/cm², which is the internal value (kg/m²) divided by 10. The default is 15 g/cm² in the GUI (150 internally). The numbers in parentheses below come from running the same pinned2.json setup headlessly with my code. "Top-edge sag" is how far the middle of the pinned edge drops below the pins, and "length" is how far the cloth hangs below the pins.

Spring constant ks

With a very low ks, the springs barely resist gravity, so the cloth stretches as far as the 10% constraint allows. The pinned top edge drops into a deep U between the two pins (sag 0.20 m), and the cloth hangs about 1.15 m below the pins, 15% longer than its rest length. Because the cloth is held up almost entirely by the hard length constraint rather than by spring forces, it never fully calms down. In my headless run it was still making small vibrations after 20 simulated seconds. With a high ks, the cloth acts like a stiff sheet. The top edge forms a shallower, smoother curve (sag 0.15 m, vs. 0.18 m at the default 5000), the cloth hangs only 1.06 m, and it stays almost rectangular. At ks = 50000 the sag drops to 0.07 m. The difference is clearest at rest. While falling, the low-ks cloth also stretches and wobbles much more before settling.

Density

Density changes the mass of every point. Gravity's acceleration doesn't depend on mass, but the springs' effect does (\(a = F_s / m\)), so a heavier cloth behaves like a cloth with weaker springs. The light cloth stays taut: its top edge is almost straight (sag 0.06 m) and it barely stretches (length 1.016 m). The heavy cloth sags deeply (sag 0.20 m, length 1.15 m) and looks almost identical to the ks = 1 case above. Both of those runs are limited only by the 10% stretch constraint. In this model mass only appears in the spring acceleration \(k_s \Delta x / m\), so what matters is the ratio ks / density. Multiplying density by some factor gives exactly the same motion as dividing ks by that factor.

Damping

Both runs below use density = 10 g/cm² and ks = 5000 N/m. Only the damping changes. The frames are taken at the same times after pressing R.

Damping = 0.011% (very low)
Damping = 0.78% (high)

Damping scales down the velocity term in the Verlet update, so it acts like air resistance. With almost no damping, energy is barely lost. The cloth whips down, overshoots, bounces back up above its rest position, and keeps swinging with waves running through it. In my headless run it still hadn't settled after 20 simulated seconds. With high damping, the cloth sinks slowly and smoothly, as if moving through syrup. It never overshoots or ripples, but it also creeps toward rest slowly: it took 8.5 s to drop below 1 cm/s, compared with 3.0 s at the default 0.2%. Damping doesn't change the final shape at all. All three settings end with the same 0.16 m top-edge sag. It only changes how the cloth gets there.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

After Verlet integration, Cloth::simulate tests every point mass against every object in collision_objects. CollisionObject::collide is virtual, so the call automatically runs Sphere::collide or Plane::collide. Both shapes follow the same pattern: detect that the mass ended up on the wrong side of the surface, pick a target point on the surface, and move the mass there starting from where it was at the previous step, slowed down by friction \(f\): \[ p_\text{new} = p_\text{last} + (1 - f)\,(p_\text{target} - p_\text{last}). \] Measuring from last_position means friction scales down how far the mass moves along the surface during this step, which is what friction should do.

  • Sphere. A mass collides if \(\|p - o\| \le r\). The target is the tangent point, found by extending the line from the center \(o\) through the mass out to the surface: \(t = o + r\,\frac{p - o}{\|p - o\|}\).
  • Plane. I compute signed distances to the plane, \(d = (p - q)\cdot n\), for both the current and the last position. If they have different signs (\(d_\text{now} \cdot d_\text{last} \le 0\)), the mass crossed the plane during this step. The tangent point drops the mass straight onto the plane, \(t = p - d_\text{now}\,n\). The target is then moved SURFACE_OFFSET back toward the side the mass came from (the sign of \(d_\text{last}\)), so the cloth rests just above the plane and works for planes approached from either side.

Cloth on a sphere with different ks

All three rest on top of the sphere without passing through it, but stiffness changes how the cloth drapes. At ks = 500 the cloth is soft and stretchy. It hugs the sphere, hangs almost straight down, and forms many narrow vertical folds. It also hangs lowest: in my headless test its lowest point was at \(y = -0.26\) m. The default 5000 forms fewer, wider folds and hangs a bit higher (\(y = -0.23\)). At ks = 50000 the cloth is stiff and resists both stretching and bending. It flares outward like a stiff tablecloth with only a few large folds, the corners lift far enough to show the underside, and it hangs highest (\(y = -0.20\)). Higher ks means fewer, broader folds and less stretch.

Cloth at rest on a plane

The cloth falls flat and stops on the plane. It rests a fraction of a millimeter above the surface: between about 0.2 and 0.7 mm in my headless run, because of SURFACE_OFFSET and friction. The grey speckles are z-fighting. The cloth and the plane are so close together that the depth buffer can't always tell which one is in front. This is a rendering artifact, not penetration: in the headless test no point mass ever went below the plane.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Checking every pair of masses against each other would cost \(O(n^2)\) per step, so I use spatial hashing. Space is divided into boxes of size \(w \times h \times t\), with \(w = 3\,\text{width}/\text{num_width_points}\), \(h = 3\,\text{height}/\text{num_height_points}\), and \(t = \max(w, h)\). That is 6 cm for the default cloth, about three grid spacings, and much larger than the 1.9 cm collision distance.

  • hash_position computes the integer box coordinates \((b_x, b_y, b_z) = (\lfloor x/w \rfloor, \lfloor y/h \rfloor, \lfloor z/t \rfloor)\) and combines them into one number, \(b_x + 113\,(b_y + 113\,b_z)\). This works like digits in base 113, so it is unique for box indices in \([-56, 56]\) (about ±3.4 m). The largest value, about 721,000, is below \(2^{24}\), so it is stored exactly in the float key. I use floor rather than truncation so that the boxes on either side of 0 are the same size.
  • build_spatial_map runs once per substep. It puts a pointer to every mass into the list for its box, creating the list the first time a box is seen.
  • self_collide looks up the mass's own box. For every other mass in the box that is closer than \(2 \cdot \text{thickness}\), it computes the push that would move the pair exactly \(2 \cdot \text{thickness}\) apart. It averages these pushes (so several close neighbors don't add up to an oversized push), divides by simulation_steps, and moves only the current mass. Dividing by simulation_steps spreads the correction over a whole frame. Verlet treats any position change as velocity, so applying a full 1 cm correction in one 1/2700 s substep would look like a 27 m/s jolt and make the cloth jitter. A mass is never compared with itself, and pinned masses are skipped.

To check that it works, I compared the same selfCollision.json setup with and without the self_collide call. I counted "overlapping pairs": masses at least three cells apart on the grid but closer than one cloth thickness in 3D, i.e. places where the cloth passes through itself.

Part 5: Shaders

Shader programs

A shader program is a pair of small programs that the GPU runs in parallel, once for each vertex and once for each pixel, which is what makes it fast enough for real time. The vertex shader runs once per vertex. It takes that vertex's attributes (position, normal, tangent, uv), transforms the position into world space (v_position) and into screen space (gl_Position), and passes the other attributes on as outputs. It can also move the vertex, which is how displacement mapping works. The GPU then rasterizes each triangle into fragments and interpolates the vertex shader's outputs across the triangle with barycentric weights. The fragment shader runs once per fragment on those interpolated values and outputs its final color out_color.

Values that are the same for the whole draw call, such as the camera position, the light, the textures and the GUI sliders, come in as uniforms. Lighting and materials are mostly the fragment shader's job: from the interpolated position and normal it computes how much light reaches the eye (diffuse, Blinn-Phong), looks up colors in textures, bends the normal (bump mapping), or reflects the view ray into an environment map (mirror). The vertex shader decides where the surface is, and the fragment shader decides what it looks like.

Blinn-Phong shading

Blinn-Phong builds the color of a point from three terms: \[ L = \underbrace{k_a I_a}_{\text{ambient}} + \underbrace{k_d \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})}_{\text{diffuse}} + \underbrace{k_s \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})^{p}}_{\text{specular}}, \qquad \mathbf{h} = \frac{\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}\|} \]

  • Ambient is a constant that stands in for light that has bounced around the room, so surfaces facing away from the light aren't pitch black. It doesn't depend on the light, normal or view direction, so on its own it gives a flat silhouette with no shape.
  • Diffuse is Lambert's law for a matte surface. Light that reaches the surface falls off with distance as \(1/r^2\) and is scaled by \(\cos\theta = \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l}\). Surfaces facing the light are bright, and surfaces turned away are dark. It doesn't depend on where the camera is.
  • Specular gives shiny highlights. The half vector \(\mathbf{h}\) points halfway between the light direction \(\mathbf{l}\) and the view direction \(\mathbf{v}\). When the normal lines up with \(\mathbf{h}\), the surface mirrors the light straight into the eye. Raising \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h}\) to a large power \(p\) keeps only the spots very close to that, which makes a small, sharp highlight that moves when the camera moves.

I used \(k_a = 0.1\), \(I_a = 1\), \(k_d = 1\) (white cloth color), \(k_s = 0.5\) and \(p = 100\). The light is at \((0.5, 2, 2)\) with intensity 3. I added a switch in Phong.frag that outputs one term at a time.

Texture mapping

Every cloth vertex has a texture coordinate \((u, v)\) from its grid position, and the rasterizer interpolates it across each triangle. Texture.frag looks up u_texture_1 at the interpolated v_uv with texture() and outputs that color. For my custom texture I replaced textures/texture_1.png with my own image.

Bump and displacement mapping

Both shaders read a height map \(h(u, v)\), the red channel of u_texture_2. Bump mapping only changes the normal used for lighting. In the fragment shader I take the slope of the height map one texel to the right and one texel up, \(dU = (h(u + 1/w, v) - h(u, v))\,k_h k_n\) and \(dV = (h(u, v + 1/h) - h(u, v))\,k_h k_n\). From these I form the local normal \(\mathbf{n}_o = (-dU, -dV, 1)\) and rotate it into world space with the tangent-bitangent-normal matrix \([\,\mathbf{t}\;\mathbf{b}\;\mathbf{n}\,]\), where \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{t}\). Then I shade with Blinn-Phong using that normal. Displacement mapping uses the same fragment shader, but its vertex shader also moves each vertex along its normal, \(\mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{n}\,h(u, v)\,k_h\), so the geometry itself changes.

For these screenshots I used the starter's gingham image (texture_4.png) as the height map, copied over texture_2.png. The settings were normal = 20, height = 0.1 for bump, and normal = 100, height = 0.01 for displacement.

Comparison. Bump mapping is cheap and looks detailed head-on, but it is a lighting trick. The surface is still smooth, so the silhouette stays perfectly round, and the bumps never block or shadow each other. Displacement mapping actually moves the surface, so the silhouette and shape change too. Its fragment shader still uses the bumped normals, so the shading looks the same as bump mapping's. The catch is that the geometry can only change at vertices. With the gingham height map and a small height, the two spheres look almost the same. The gingham's red channel is high in both the white and the red squares (90% of pixels fall between 0.56 and 0.96), so displacement mostly inflates the sphere evenly instead of making visible bumps.

Effect of sphere mesh coarseness (-o 16 -a 16 vs. -o 128 -a 128)

At the gingham settings above, the coarse and fine spheres looked almost identical. The height varies by only about 0.4 × 0.01 = 0.004 m on a 0.4 m wide sphere, which is a couple of pixels on screen. So I switched to a height map with strong contrast and larger features: texture_3.png, bricks with dark mortar. I used the spec's settings of normal = 100, height = 0.02 for both shaders.

These images were rendered offscreen by a small standalone OpenGL program. It loads my Bump.vert/.frag and Displacement.vert/.frag files unchanged, builds the sphere with the starter's SphereMesh code (16×16 or 128×128, drawn radius 0.2 × 0.92), and uses the simulator's light. They are not screenshots from clothsim, and the cloth isn't drawn, so the sphere is fully visible.

  • Bump mapping barely changes. The brick pattern looks the same at both resolutions, because the height map is read at every pixel. The only visible difference is the outline, which is a polygon at 16×16 and round at 128×128.
  • Displacement depends heavily on resolution. At 16×16 the vertex shader reads the height map at only 17×17 points, which is fewer than one vertex per brick. The sphere becomes a lumpy, faceted shape whose lumps don't line up with the bricks, while the lighting still shows fine bricks the geometry doesn't have. At 128×128 there are several vertices per brick, so the geometry follows the pattern. The outline steps in at the mortar grooves and the shape matches the shading.
  • Takeaway: displacement is only as detailed as the mesh, so it needs a fine mesh, which costs more vertices. Bump mapping gives per-pixel detail on any mesh but can't change the silhouette.

Environment-mapped reflections (mirror)

Mirror.frag treats the surface as a perfect mirror. It computes the direction from the surface point to the camera, \(\mathbf{w}_o = \text{normalize}(\text{cam} - \mathbf{p})\), and reflects it about the normal, \(\mathbf{w}_i = 2(\mathbf{w}_o\cdot\mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{n} - \mathbf{w}_o\). Then it looks up the cubemap in direction \(\mathbf{w}_i\). There is no light or shading term: the color is whatever the environment shows in the reflected direction. The method ignores anything in the scene that could block the reflected ray.
